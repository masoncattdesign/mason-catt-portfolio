Windows Visual
Systems & Assets

Six workstreams inside Windows Design Systems. They start from one problem: Windows has more surfaces than any one person can hand-craft, and the quality bar is set at the level of a single corner radius. Everything here is an attempt to hold that bar at a scale where hand-craft runs out. Catch what slips, make expression testable, and build the tools that let other people do both.

Product accuracy at scale
01

Marketing Compendiums

Marketing needs assets accurate to shipped product across the full landscape of features and surfaces, and that accuracy decays constantly because product keeps moving and nothing is watching the gap. I audited the compendium against product's plan of record, surface by surface, and the gaps kept turning up. Corners that did not match the spec. Materials applied inconsistently across shells. Color drift between what was designed and what shipped. States that got cut somewhere between design and build and were never reconciled. What came out of it is not a faster way to make assets one at a time. It is the thing that catches the drift.

1 / 3
The library

The compendium

The full landscape of Windows product and feature surfaces, held accurate to what actually shipped. It is the reference the rest of the marketing work is built from.

The response

Component, template and screen libraries

Built so a campaign surface gets assembled from parts that are already correct, rather than produced from scratch and checked afterwards. Design Systems and product design partners now pull from the same libraries, which is not what they were scoped for.

The mechanism

The sync pipeline

Keeps product-accurate screens and flows in step between marketing and product as the product moves, so the gap closes on a schedule instead of whenever somebody notices.

Components and patterns
02

Component Libraries

I build and maintain production-aligned Figma libraries. They go to product design partners who carry them through to implementation, so the bar is that a designer can build a real screen without inventing anything. That bar is where most kits fall down: a kit gives you buttons and inputs, a system gives you the compositions people actually need, so nobody reassembles the same layout from primitives every time.

1 / 2
The library

Production-aligned components

Variants, states and tokens built to match what engineering will actually ship, plus the documentation and adoption workflow that makes them get used rather than admired.

The open problem

Pattern coverage

The newer system needs to be at least on par with the library it replaces, and it is short on patterns people can take off the shelf and use. I have been working the gap from where people are actually blocked rather than from what looks incomplete on a coverage chart.

Color systems
03

Expressive Assets

Nobody knows yet what expression should look like across Windows, and you cannot answer that by looking at three icons. You have to see a direction across the whole family, in every mode, under identical conditions, or you are comparing a good mockup to a bad one instead of comparing two ideas. So the first real problem was not picking a style. It was building the thing that makes styles comparable, and everything below is in service of that.

1 / 4
The invisible part

The color model

Built on OKLCH rather than HSL or LAB, because perceptual uniformity is the whole point: a generated ramp has to stay even across the hue wheel or an automated pass makes some icons look heavy and others washed out. Underneath sits a palette and mode system of ten color modes across Base, Chromatic, Accented and Contrasting.

What it produces

Test directions

Flat, Outline, Roles, Tinted, Soft, Dichromic and Acrylic, set against the Default originals and all driven from a single seed. These are test directions, not proposals. They exist so the team can see what the range actually looks like before anyone commits, and nothing in them is settled. Testing is early and has not surfaced much yet.

Not bolted on

Accessibility modes

High Contrast and Dichromic are generated from the same model as everything else, which is the only way they stay correct as the set grows. High contrast forces the hardest question in the whole system: does this asset survive on structure alone?

The vocabulary

Asset anatomy

Before anything can be themed consistently it has to be describable. Whole, base, emblem, glyph, and the rule that a part is a set of shapes sharing a ramp, not a region. Deliberately small, and argued out in Teams.

Illustration
04

Windows Illustrations

The illustration work spun out of expressive assets into its own system. It shares the color role model and will share the same contract with the underlying design system, but illustration has problems icons do not: it is looser, it carries more meaning per asset, and a library of finished drawings cannot be generated from. So the system is built as typed pieces rather than as a folder of outputs, and a single asset now has to land on both light and dark surfaces instead of shipping as a pair.

1 / 2
The structure

The shape taxonomy

Base shapes are the backgrounds. Secondary shapes lay over them and stay movable and scalable. Tertiary shapes are small emblem-like containers, either the element itself or a holder for a system icon. Typing the pieces this way is what makes generation possible later.

The approach

Pilot set and guidelines

Twelve interim assets as the pilot: perfect those first, use them to populate the guidelines, then rebuild the back catalog from the simplified principles rather than redrawing it. The interim direction runs bolder and more saturated than the current shipped set, with cooler, darker neutrals.

Plugins and generators
05

Asset Tooling

None of the systems above survive contact with real scale unless somebody builds the thing that operates them. This is where assets get made, browsed and altered: one place to see everything that exists, one place to change it, and generators that assemble new work from the pieces rather than starting a drawing from nothing. The tools are not the achievement. They are what makes the achievement usable by anyone other than me.

Theming past the icon
06

Theming & Personalization

Theming an icon set is the tractable version of the problem. The real question is what happens when a direction has to hold across everything on screen at once: icons, illustration, interface and wallpaper, together, without the result feeling like a costume over the top of Windows. That is a harder thing to judge by description, so the work here is about making it visible enough to argue about.

1 / 2
The instrument

The Customizer, for theming

Seen from the theming end: pick a direction, push it through a whole family at once, and see it resolve under the same conditions every time. That last part is what makes two candidate directions actually comparable instead of one being a better mockup than the other.

The pilot

Desktop Themer

Takes the same theming model out past the icon set and onto a prospective desktop: illustration, interface surfaces and wallpaper all moving together under one direction. It is where you find out whether a direction is actually a direction, or just a nice icon set.
